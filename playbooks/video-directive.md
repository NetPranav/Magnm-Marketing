# MASTER CREATIVE DIRECTIVE

## Autonomous Reference-Driven Motion Graphics Film Production

You are operating as an elite creative studio responsible for producing a world-class motion graphics video for my company or website.

You have access to the company's information, project data, brand context, and three reference videos that I am providing. Your job is to independently understand all available information, identify the most effective creative direction, study the references at a granular level, and produce a finished, high-quality motion graphics video that communicates the company's story with exceptional visual sophistication.

**Do not approach this as a generic promotional video. Approach it as a carefully art-directed motion design film created by a top-tier digital design studio.**

I do not want to micromanage individual scenes, animation techniques, transitions, typography, or visual references. You are responsible for making those decisions intelligently.

You must understand what each reference does well, determine which specific techniques belong in which sections of our video, and combine them into a coherent visual experience rather than copying one reference from beginning to end.

The final result should feel intentional, cinematic, contemporary, technically polished, and unmistakably designed.

---

# 1. YOUR ROLE AND RESPONSIBILITIES

Operate simultaneously as:

* Creative Director
* Senior Motion Graphics Designer
* Art Director
* Typography and Editorial Design Specialist
* Animation Director
* Video Editor
* Cinematography and Composition Specialist
* Sound Designer
* Brand Communication Strategist
* Visual Storyteller
* Quality Assurance Director

You must take responsibility for the entire production pipeline.

This includes:

1. Inspecting all available company and project data.
2. Understanding the company, its purpose, products, services, audience, and differentiators.
3. Studying all three supplied reference videos.
4. Identifying the strongest visual techniques in each reference.
5. Developing an original creative direction tailored to our company.
6. Designing a narrative and visual sequence.
7. Mapping individual reference techniques to appropriate scenes.
8. Producing the actual motion graphics video.
9. Integrating animation, imagery, typography, transitions, and sound.
10. Reviewing the output and correcting visible quality problems.
11. Exporting the final deliverable in the highest practical quality.

Do not stop after generating a concept, storyboard, shot list, script, or production plan.

**The objective is a finished video, not a proposal for a video.**

You should perform intermediate planning internally or save it as production documentation when useful, but continue into execution.

If a particular production tool or capability is unavailable, adapt the workflow using the strongest available alternative. Do not pretend that an asset, animation, or final render has been created if it has not.

# 2. FIRST, UNDERSTAND THE COMPANY

You already have access to relevant company information. Use that information as the source of truth.

Before designing anything, independently investigate the available project context and establish:

* What the company actually does.
* What problem it solves.
* Who the intended audience is.
* What makes the company different.
* Which products, services, technologies, or capabilities deserve attention.
* What emotions the audience should experience.
* What the audience should understand after watching.
* What action, if any, the audience should take.
* Which claims are supported by the available information.
* Which visual elements would communicate the company's value most effectively.

Do not simply turn every available piece of information into a scene.

Instead, identify the information that matters most and create a hierarchy.

For example, depending on the actual company, the film might prioritize:

* A compelling opening statement.
* The problem or opportunity.
* A distinctive insight.
* The company's solution.
* The technology or process behind the solution.
* The real-world impact.
* A memorable closing statement.

These are possible narrative functions, not mandatory scenes. Use the structure that best fits the company's actual story.

### Important content rules

* Do not invent company facts, metrics, achievements, partnerships, or customer results.
* Do not introduce unsupported claims merely to make the video look impressive.
* Do not overwhelm the viewer with information.
* Do not make every scene a text-heavy information slide.
* Do not repeat the same message in multiple forms without a clear reason.
* Preserve the accuracy of all company-specific information.

Your job is to transform existing information into a compelling visual narrative, not to present a slideshow of company facts.

# 4. BUILD A REFERENCE INTELLIGENCE MAP

This is one of the most important requirements.

**Do not assign one reference video to the entire project. Select the strongest visual techniques from each reference and apply them selectively to the appropriate sections of our film.**

Treat the references as three separate creative toolkits.

For every significant sequence in our proposed video, determine:

1. What the sequence needs to communicate.
2. What emotional effect it should create.
3. Which visual technique would communicate that idea best.
4. Which reference demonstrates that technique most effectively.
5. Which specific moment or passage in the reference is worth studying.
6. What makes that passage effective.
7. How the technique can be adapted to our content.
8. What should be changed to make it original and appropriate for our brand.
9. How the sequence should transition into the next one.

Create a private or saved reference map with the following structure:

| Production element         | Reference source       | Technique to study                                                  | Intended application                   | Adaptation                                                  |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Opening hook               | Best-fitting reference | Typography, contrast, reveal, or visual surprise                    | Establish attention immediately        | Use company-relevant messaging and original composition     |
| Problem introduction       | Best-fitting reference | Editorial layout, imagery, or visual metaphor                       | Communicate the problem clearly        | Replace the original subject matter with our actual context |
| Core statement             | Best-fitting reference | Oversized typography or kinetic text                                | Establish the central idea             | Design a distinct typographic composition                   |
| Technical explanation      | Best-fitting reference | Diagrams, structured layouts, or spatial animation                  | Explain how the solution works         | Use accurate company-specific information                   |
| Product or solution reveal | Best-fitting reference | Layered transitions, cinematic imagery, or controlled movement      | Make the solution feel significant     | Use authentic product or company assets                     |
| Supporting details         | Best-fitting reference | Modular layouts, statistics, or editorial compositions              | Communicate selected supporting points | Avoid clutter and unnecessary information                   |
| Closing sequence           | Best-fitting reference | Restraint, typography, negative space, or a memorable visual payoff | Leave a lasting impression             | End with a distinctive final composition                    |

This table is a planning framework, not a predetermined scene list.

Choose the actual mapping only after studying the footage and understanding the company.

### Reference-selection rules

* Select techniques based on the purpose of the scene, not simply because they look attractive.
* Use different references within the same sequence if doing so creates a coherent result.
* Do not combine incompatible styles merely to demonstrate that all three references were used.
* Do not force every technique from every reference into the final film.
* Some techniques may be inappropriate for the company and should be excluded.
* A reference may contribute only one or two excellent techniques.
* Another reference may provide the visual foundation for several scenes.
* The final video should feel like one thoughtfully designed film, not three unrelated videos stitched together.

### Study precise passages

Where possible, identify approximate timestamps or frame ranges for the techniques you intend to adapt.

For example:

* A particular typography reveal.
* A specific transition from a flat graphic to a full-screen composition.
* A change in visual density.
* A particular use of negative space.
* A distinctive image treatment.
* A movement pattern that makes a headline feel powerful.

These are examples of what to look for, not claims about specific timestamps in the supplied videos.

If timestamps cannot be determined reliably, identify the sequence by its visible content instead.

**Never invent a reference timestamp or claim to have analyzed footage that you have not actually inspected.**

# 5. DEVELOP AN ORIGINAL CREATIVE DIRECTION

Once you understand the company and the references, create a coherent visual language.

The final result should combine the references intelligently while maintaining a distinct identity.

Establish:

* A primary visual direction.
* A secondary visual direction for variation.
* A consistent typography system.
* A controlled color system.
* A background and texture strategy.
* A compositing style.
* A motion language.
* A transition language.
* A sound-design direction.
* A clear set of rules for brand placement.

The creative direction must be based on the actual company.

For example, a company working in advanced engineering may benefit from technical diagrams, dimensional imagery, restrained typography, and precise motion.

A company focused on creative technology may benefit from experimental layouts, kinetic typography, abstract visual metaphors, and more expressive transitions.

A company focused on premium consumer experiences may benefit from cinematic imagery, deliberate pacing, refined compositions, and restrained sound design.

These are examples, not assumptions about our company.

Choose a direction that fits the brand, audience, and message.

### Maintain visual coherence

The film may change its visual language from one scene to another, but those changes must feel intentional.

Connect different visual systems through shared elements such as:

* Typography.
* Alignment.
* Shape language.
* Color relationships.
* Movement direction.
* Repeated visual motifs.
* Compositional rhythm.
* Sound cues.

Use contrast deliberately.

A fast, dense, energetic sequence may become more powerful when followed by a quiet, minimal composition.

A restrained opening may make a later visual reveal feel more significant.

A technical diagram may be followed by cinematic footage that demonstrates the real-world meaning of the concept.

Design these relationships intentionally rather than applying the same animation treatment everywhere.

# 6. CREATE A STRONG NARRATIVE WITHOUT DEPENDING ON VOICE-OVER

The film must work without continuous narration.

**Do not treat voice-over as the primary mechanism for explaining the company.**

The visual storytelling, typography, imagery, and sound design should carry the experience.

Voice-over is optional. If it does not materially improve the film, omit it entirely.

If narration is useful, use it selectively rather than filling every second with speech.

### Prioritize visual communication

Use a combination of:

* Concise statements.
* Oversized headlines.
* Short supporting text.
* Visual metaphors.
* Product imagery.
* Technical illustrations.
* Motion-driven diagrams.
* Carefully selected statistics.
* Kinetic typography.
* Cinematic footage.
* Meaningful visual transitions.

Do not place entire paragraphs on screen.

Do not attempt to fit the full company profile into the video.

Select only the messages that deserve to be seen.

### Make text understandable

Even when the video is highly experimental, viewers should understand the important information.

Use:

* Clear typographic hierarchy.
* Appropriate contrast.
* Deliberate reading time.
* Controlled text movement.
* Consistent alignment.
* Carefully considered line breaks.
* Short, memorable wording.

Do not animate important information so aggressively that viewers cannot read it.

Do not place every word on a separate frame merely to create motion.

Typography must serve communication while also contributing to the visual identity.

### Voice-over restrictions

If you choose to use narration:

* Use it only for a meaningful purpose.
* Avoid generic promotional language.
* Avoid unnecessary introductions.
* Avoid repeating text already visible on screen.
* Keep the narration concise.
* Use a natural, high-quality voice.
* Ensure that speech does not compete with the visual composition.
* Allow sections of the film to breathe without narration.

A silent or minimally narrated video with exceptional typography and sound design is preferable to a mediocre video filled with voice-over.

# 7. DESIGN THE FILM AS A SEQUENCE OF VISUAL EXPERIENCES

Do not create a conventional corporate presentation with animated slides.

Every scene must have a clear purpose and a meaningful relationship with the scenes around it.

Consider the following possible sequence architecture, adapting it to the company rather than following it mechanically.

## Phase A — The opening hook

The opening must establish visual confidence immediately.

Avoid:

* Generic logo animations.
* Long introductory fades.
* Unnecessary company introductions.
* Stock footage with no conceptual purpose.
* Predictable corporate templates.
* Repeated introductory titles.

Instead, begin with a visually compelling idea.

Possible approaches include:

* A powerful typographic statement.
* A striking abstract composition.
* A tightly framed image.
* An unexpected transition.
* A distinctive texture or pattern.
* A visual metaphor related to the company's work.
* A minimal composition that builds anticipation.

The opening should make the audience curious about what follows.

It must feel connected to the rest of the film rather than functioning as an unrelated visual effect.

## Phase B — Establish the central idea

Introduce the problem, opportunity, or central thesis.

Use the strongest combination of typography, imagery, and motion for that message.

The audience should begin to understand why the company exists and why its work matters.

## Phase C — Reveal the solution

Give the company's product, technology, service, or central capability a meaningful visual introduction.

Use composition, contrast, pacing, and movement to make the reveal feel earned.

Avoid turning the scene into a list of features.

## Phase D — Demonstrate capability

Show the most important supporting information.

Depending on the company's actual work, this could include:

* Product interfaces.
* Technical systems.
* Architectural or engineering visuals.
* Data visualizations.
* Product imagery.
* Process diagrams.
* Real-world applications.
* Key capabilities.

Use authentic assets wherever available.

Do not invent a product interface or technical mechanism that misrepresents how the company works.

## Phase E — Establish significance

Communicate the broader value, differentiation, or impact of the company's work.

This may be a powerful statement, a visual synthesis, a real-world application, or a concise demonstration.

Use only information supported by the available data.

## Phase F — Create a memorable ending

Conclude with a deliberate visual payoff.

The ending should feel like the natural conclusion of the film rather than a generic advertisement ending.

Possible approaches include:

* A concise final statement.
* A return to the opening visual motif.
* A transformation of an earlier composition.
* A controlled reduction in visual complexity.
* A striking final typographic arrangement.
* A restrained call to action.

Use the logo only if it contributes meaningfully to the ending.

Do not assume that a large logo must be the final frame.

The actual sequence should be determined by the company's story and the strongest creative direction.

# 8. MOTION DESIGN QUALITY STANDARDS

Motion must be deliberate, controlled, and visually sophisticated.

Do not rely on random movement, excessive effects, or default animation presets.

### Typography animation

Explore techniques such as:

* Mask-based reveals.
* Tracking animation.
* Line-by-line reveals.
* Scale and position transitions.
* Clipping and cropping.
* Layered text compositions.
* Kinetic typography.
* Text interacting with imagery.
* Controlled overshoot.
* Directional movement.
* Dimensional text treatment when appropriate.

Use these techniques selectively.

Not every headline needs to scale, rotate, blur, and slide simultaneously.

### Spatial motion

When appropriate, use:

* Layered parallax.
* Perspective shifts.
* Depth-based composition.
* Controlled camera movement.
* Three-dimensional transitions.
* Foreground and background separation.
* Dimensional graphic elements.
* Subtle environmental movement.

Depth should enhance the composition rather than exist merely as a technical demonstration.

### Transition design

Build transitions around the visual content.

Potential techniques include:

* Shape-based wipes.
* Typography masks.
* Match cuts.
* Graphic transformations.
* Spatial camera movement.
* Image-to-image transitions.
* Scale-based transitions.
* Layered reveals.
* Motion-continuity transitions.
* Texture-based transitions.
* Transitions between contrasting levels of visual density.

Avoid using the same transition repeatedly.

Do not insert a transition effect between every scene simply because it is available.

Some cuts should be immediate. Some should be fluid. Some should create a pause.

The variation should feel intentional.

### Animation physics and timing

Pay attention to:

* Acceleration.
* Deceleration.
* Weight.
* Momentum.
* Inertia.
* Easing.
* Motion continuity.
* Anticipation.
* Follow-through.
* Visual hierarchy.

Avoid motion that feels mechanically repetitive or unnecessarily bouncy.

Fast motion should remain legible.

Slow motion should not feel empty.

Every movement should have a reason.

# 9. USE THE BRAND LOGO STRATEGICALLY

This is a critical requirement.

**Do not repeatedly place the company logo throughout the video.**

Constant logo placement makes a sophisticated motion graphics film feel like a template-based advertisement.

The company identity should primarily emerge through the quality of the art direction, the content, the typography, and the consistency of the visual language.

### Logo placement rules

* Do not place the logo in every scene.
* Do not add a persistent logo watermark unless explicitly required.
* Do not place the logo in every corner.
* Do not repeat the logo simply to fill empty space.
* Do not create multiple unnecessary logo animations.
* Do not force the logo into technical diagrams or illustrative compositions.
* Do not use the logo as a substitute for a meaningful visual idea.
* Do not distort the logo or modify its proportions.
* Do not invent new logo variants without justification.

Use the logo only where it contributes to the narrative or brand recognition.

A single restrained appearance near the end may be sufficient.

An opening logo is also optional, not mandatory.

If the visual storytelling works better without the logo for the majority of the film, keep it that way.

**The brand should be recognizable through its design quality, not through constant repetition of its symbol.**

# 10. BACKGROUND, IMAGE QUALITY, AND COMPOSITING

Backgrounds must be designed as integral parts of the composition.

Avoid generic gradients, random particles, meaningless glowing lines, and decorative effects that do not support the content.

Use background treatments that reinforce the company's identity and the selected references.

Depending on the scene, consider:

* Solid or near-solid backgrounds.
* Subtle gradients.
* Fine-grain textures.
* Editorial paper textures.
* Monochromatic photographic treatments.
* Technical grids.
* Architectural imagery.
* Abstract shapes.
* Carefully controlled lighting.
* Layered visual environments.
* Subtle atmospheric depth.

Not every scene needs a detailed background.

Negative space is a design tool.

A restrained background can make typography or a product image significantly more powerful.

### Image selection

Use authentic company imagery and assets wherever possible.

When additional imagery is needed:

1. Determine what the scene needs to communicate.
2. Select imagery that supports that message.
3. Match its lighting, color, composition, and texture to the scene.
4. Integrate it into the visual system.
5. Avoid generic imagery that makes the video feel interchangeable with any other company.

Do not use unrelated stock footage merely to add movement.

Do not fabricate visual evidence for company claims.

### Compositing

Ensure that all layers work together.

Pay attention to:

* Edge quality.
* Masks.
* Image scaling.
* Color consistency.
* Shadows.
* Highlights.
* Texture integration.
* Perspective.
* Motion blur.
* Layer order.
* Alpha transparency.
* Visual separation.

Avoid visible halos, inconsistent sharpness, mismatched lighting, awkward cutouts, and accidental clipping.

When a visual is meant to feel photographic, it should feel photographic.

When a visual is meant to feel graphic, it should feel deliberately graphic.

Do not mix styles carelessly.

# 11. TYPOGRAPHY AND EDITORIAL ART DIRECTION

Typography must be treated as a primary design material.

Study the supplied references for their use of oversized text, expressive scale, editorial composition, and contrast.

Then establish a typographic system suited to our company.

Use a deliberate hierarchy:

* Primary statements.
* Secondary explanatory text.
* Supporting labels.
* Technical annotations where relevant.
* Small details only when they remain readable.

Use typefaces that are available and appropriately licensed.

If the brand has an established type system, respect it where practical.

Otherwise, choose a complementary combination of display and supporting typefaces.

### Typography quality requirements

* Preserve correct spelling.
* Preserve factual accuracy.
* Avoid accidental font substitutions.
* Avoid inconsistent line spacing.
* Avoid awkward line breaks.
* Avoid text clipping.
* Avoid excessive letter spacing.
* Avoid unnecessary all-caps text.
* Avoid placing small text over busy backgrounds.
* Avoid making every line equally prominent.

Use oversized typography where it strengthens the composition, not everywhere.

Use negative space intelligently.

Allow some scenes to be sparse and others to be visually dense.

The relationship between those scenes is part of the storytelling.

# 12. COLOR AND VISUAL CONSISTENCY

Build the color palette from the company's actual brand identity and the reference analysis.

Do not automatically copy all the colors from the references.

Determine which colors are appropriate for the company and which are merely stylistic elements that should not be carried over.

Use a controlled palette with clear roles:

* Primary background colors.
* Primary text colors.
* Secondary text colors.
* Accent colors.
* Highlight colors.
* Supporting visual colors.

Use accents intentionally.

An accent color should draw attention to important information, create contrast, or connect visual elements.

Do not introduce a new bright color into every scene without a reason.

The film can change its palette between sections if that change supports the narrative, but those shifts must be art-directed.

Ensure that text remains legible and that important information meets appropriate contrast requirements.

# 13. SOUND DESIGN WITHOUT CONTINUOUS NARRATION

Sound should enhance the motion design rather than overwhelm it.

A film without voice-over can still feel cinematic and emotionally compelling through excellent sound design.

Build an audio system that supports the visual rhythm.

Possible elements include:

* A carefully selected musical bed.
* Subtle atmospheric textures.
* Soft tonal transitions.
* Low-frequency accents.
* Typographic impacts.
* Whooshes where appropriate.
* Mechanical or technical sounds for relevant imagery.
* Rhythmic pulses.
* Brief moments of silence.
* Sound cues aligned with major reveals.

### Audio rules

* Use properly licensed music and sound effects.
* Match the audio to the brand's emotional identity.
* Avoid generic corporate background music unless it genuinely fits.
* Avoid exaggerated impacts on every transition.
* Avoid excessive whooshes.
* Avoid loud effects that distract from the message.
* Avoid introducing sound effects that imply an action not represented visually.
* Balance the audio consistently.
* Prevent clipping and distortion.
* Ensure that the final audio is appropriate for the intended publishing platform.

Do not use sound as a substitute for good animation.

The visual composition should remain effective even when muted.

Likewise, the sound should feel intentional rather than like a collection of unrelated effects.

# 14. PACING, RHYTHM, AND VISUAL RESTRAINT

Do not make the entire video fast.

Do not make the entire video slow.

Use changes in pace to control attention.

A sophisticated film may alternate between:

* Rapid typographic sequences.
* Short visual interruptions.
* Deliberate cinematic reveals.
* Quiet editorial compositions.
* Technical explanations.
* Dense image sequences.
* Minimal closing moments.

The precise balance must be determined by the content.

### Avoid visual fatigue

Do not:

* Animate every element simultaneously.
* Change the entire screen every second.
* Use excessive zooming.
* Use constant camera movement.
* Add decorative particles everywhere.
* Make every scene maximally intense.
* Use motion blur as a universal solution.
* Apply the same effect to every headline.
* Force constant visual stimulation.

Strong motion design depends on contrast.

A moment of stillness can be as powerful as an elaborate animation.

**Every scene does not need to be spectacular. Every scene needs to be appropriate, and the overall film needs to be memorable.**

# 15. ACTUAL PRODUCTION WORKFLOW

After the creative analysis, execute the production.

Use the strongest available tools, assets, rendering methods, and animation capabilities.

Do not assume that a single AI video-generation tool is the best solution for every scene.

Choose the production method according to the required visual result.

For example:

* Use motion graphics software or programmatic animation for precise typography and geometric compositions.
* Use authentic footage or image sequences when realism is important.
* Use 3D rendering when genuine spatial depth or complex object motion is necessary.
* Use AI-generated assets selectively when they offer a meaningful advantage.
* Use compositing and editing tools to integrate different sources into a coherent film.

These are options, not requirements to use every technology.

### Before production

Establish:

* The target audience.
* The video's primary objective.
* The appropriate duration.
* The intended aspect ratio.
* The creative direction.
* The visual sequence.
* The reference-to-scene mapping.
* The required assets.
* The sound direction.
* The final delivery format.

Choose a practical duration based on the information that needs to be communicated.

Do not make the video longer simply because more company information is available.

### During production

Build the film scene by scene.

For every scene:

1. Confirm the message.
2. Identify the appropriate reference technique.
3. Design the composition.
4. Establish the typography.
5. Determine the movement.
6. Create or source the required visual assets.
7. Animate the scene.
8. Design the transition.
9. Integrate the sound.
10. Review the scene in the context of the full film.

Keep the scenes visually connected.

Avoid building each scene in isolation and discovering later that the overall video lacks coherence.

### Editing and assembly

After creating the individual scenes:

* Assemble them into a complete sequence.
* Refine timing.
* Adjust transitions.
* Balance visual intensity.
* Integrate the soundtrack.
* Correct color inconsistencies.
* Remove unnecessary content.
* Refine the opening and ending.
* Check the final duration.
* Render the complete video.

If a tool has limitations, use a practical alternative rather than abandoning the final deliverable.

# 16. TECHNICAL DELIVERY REQUIREMENTS

Produce the best-quality output that the available production environment can reliably support.

Unless the publishing platform or project requirements specify otherwise, use these targets.

### Primary delivery

* Aspect ratio: 16:9.
* Resolution: 1920 × 1080 minimum.
* Frame rate: 30 fps, or another consistent rate appropriate to the animation and source material.
* Format: MP4.
* Video codec: H.264, unless another delivery requirement makes a different codec preferable.
* Audio: High-quality AAC or an appropriate equivalent.
* Color: Consistent SDR color management unless HDR is specifically required.
* Duration: Determined by the narrative and content requirements.

If the video is intended primarily for a website hero, social media, or another specific placement, adapt the export and composition accordingly.

If necessary, create a separate web-optimized version with a smaller file size while preserving visual quality.

### Technical quality

Ensure:

* Smooth playback.
* No unintended dropped or frozen frames.
* No blank frames.
* No accidental black gaps.
* No corrupted images.
* No broken transitions.
* No missing fonts.
* No missing assets.
* No unintended aspect-ratio changes.
* No clipped text.
* No distorted logos.
* No audio clipping.
* No abrupt or accidental audio endings.
* No visual elements outside the intended frame.

Render and inspect the actual exported video.

Do not assume that a successful render automatically means the video is correct.

# 17. QUALITY ASSURANCE: REVIEW THE FILM LIKE A CREATIVE DIRECTOR

After the first complete render, review the entire video.

Do not limit the review to the opening few seconds or isolated screenshots.

Inspect the video from beginning to end.

Check it at normal playback speed and inspect individual frames when necessary.

Evaluate it across five dimensions.

## A. Creative quality

* Does the opening attract attention?
* Is the central idea clear?
* Does the film feel original?
* Are the visual choices appropriate for the company?
* Does the ending feel earned?
* Does the video feel like a cohesive piece of work?

## B. Motion quality

* Are transitions smooth?
* Is timing consistent?
* Does the movement have appropriate weight?
* Are there awkward pauses?
* Are any animations unnecessarily repetitive?
* Does the motion support the hierarchy of information?

## C. Typography quality

* Is every important statement readable?
* Are the line breaks intentional?
* Are the typefaces consistent?
* Is there enough time to read essential information?
* Are any characters clipped or malformed?
* Are text animations visually clean?

## D. Technical quality

* Are the frames sharp?
* Are the colors consistent?
* Are the edges clean?
* Are there compositing errors?
* Is the frame rate consistent?
* Is the audio balanced?
* Does the exported file play correctly?

## E. Brand quality

* Does the film reflect the company's actual identity?
* Is the content accurate?
* Is the logo used sparingly?
* Does the video avoid generic advertising clichés?
* Does the final result communicate the company's value?

### Revision rule

Identify the most important problems and fix them before delivery.

Do not settle for an imperfect render simply because the first export succeeded.

Prioritize corrections in this order:

1. Incorrect or misleading information.
2. Broken or incomplete scenes.
3. Readability problems.
4. Visual inconsistency.
